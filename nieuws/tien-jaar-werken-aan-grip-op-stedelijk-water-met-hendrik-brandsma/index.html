Ten years of working to get a grip on urban water with Hendrik Brandsma

A handover on paper is something different from implementation in the field

This year Tijhuis Ingenieurs turns 30. A good moment to look back at projects that make a difference. For project manager Hendrik Brandsma, one of those projects began as early as 2016: the Transfer of Urban Water (Overdracht Stedelijk Water) in the municipality of De Fryske Marren. In that same year, municipalities and Wetterskip Fryslân made agreements under the Transfer of Urban Water (OSW). The OSW set out agreements on the management and maintenance of watercourses in urban areas. It came with a clear deadline: any maintenance backlog had to be cleared before 31 December 2025.

What seemed a clear agreement on paper proved a more complex task in practice, spread across several municipalities, with differing starting points and limited capacity. How do you deal with differences between theoretical standards and the reality on the ground? What appeared to start as a supplement to a dredging plan in the municipality of De Fryske Marren grew into a long-term project spanning several municipalities, with overview, structure, technical precision and collaboration at its core.

From standard to nuance: sharpening the starting points

The first challenge he focused on lay not in implementation, but in interpreting the agreements. The handover document stated, for example, that a watercourse had to be 50 centimetres deep. But what does that mean in practice? When do you meet the standard, and when does a situation still call for action?

In addition, Hendrik saw that there was a question over whether only the minimum standard had to be met, or whether it would be wiser to build in a maintenance depth that is also future-proof. By discussing the starting points afresh together with the municipalities and the Wetterskip, and testing them against practice, uniform starting points were established in the process. Not out of debate, but out of the shared goal: a well-functioning, future-proof water system.

Bringing structure where time is lacking

Many municipalities wanted to carry out the handover carefully, but lacked the time or the tools to make the task concrete. As the 2025 deadline approached, the file remained untouched in some places. That was precisely where Hendrik took the initiative.

Using GIS analyses and targeted field measurements, it was mapped out exactly which watercourses already complied and where there was a maintenance backlog. He translated these insights into clear schedules per village or neighbourhood. By bringing all the data together in our own software W.I.T., an overview emerged: what do we do when, with what budget and with what contribution from the water board? What initially felt abstract and extensive thus became feasible and manageable.

The stable factor in a long-running project

A project lasting almost ten years calls for continuity. During that period several project managers at Wetterskip Fryslân came and went, while Hendrik remained the constant factor. He safeguarded knowledge within Tijhuis Ingenieurs, recorded decisions and ensured that new participants quickly gained insight into the history and the agreements made.

When the pressure increased in the final years, a solid foundation was therefore already in place. Municipalities could take targeted decisions and prepare projects more quickly. In the concluding phase the pace was stepped up and some sizeable dredging works were carried out, including projects of around 20,000 m³ within a few months. Thanks to the earlier preparation and clear direction, this phase too could be completed efficiently and carefully, and wrapped up together with the Wetterskip.

More than maintenance: investing in a robust water system

Under Hendrik's guidance, the handover grew into more than an administrative agreement. Watercourses were assessed systematically, the maintenance backlog was tackled and lasting insight was gained into the maintenance condition of urban water systems.

Thanks to the OSW, municipalities and the Wetterskip jointly invested in better water flow, data management, water quality and, with them, biodiversity. This means that not only have the 2016 agreements been met, but real work has been done towards a robust, future-proof water system. That fits seamlessly with what Tijhuis Ingenieurs stands for: moving forward sustainably, together.

What this project shows

For Hendrik, this project was more than an assignment. It was a long-term responsibility with a clear goal on the horizon. By acting proactively, connecting parties and staying sharp on the technical content, he made sure the handover did not stall but was actually completed.

The Overdracht Stedelijk Water (Urban Water Handover) shows what Tijhuis Ingenieurs is good at: translating complex agreements into workable plans, turning data into clear choices, and not only preparing projects but also delivering them.

Where do the opportunities for nature-friendly banks lie?

Our clients want to create more nature-friendly banks. Understandably so, because they contribute to cleaner water, greater biodiversity and a more attractive living environment. But where do you start?

Not every bank is suitable, and assessing all possible locations manually takes a great deal of time. That is why Tijhuis Ingenieurs developed an NVO tool: a tool that makes it quicker to identify promising locations for nature-friendly banks. The tool uses data that is often already available, such as profile data, elevation data, ownership information, a tree map and the BGT (Dutch large-scale topographic map) for the location of buildings and roads.

We spoke about this with Eline van Wijk, project manager and sustainability specialist at Tijhuis Ingenieurs. She is closely involved in the development and application of the tool. As one of the driving forces in the field of nature-friendly banks, she sees how many opportunities remain to link existing management tasks to greater biodiversity along the water.

Above all, it is a way to provide direction more quickly.

More nature along the water

A nature-friendly bank is more than a nicer edge along the water. By creating a gradual transition between land and water, space is created for plants and animals that depend on shallow water. Think of aquatic and bank plants, insects, amphibians and small mammals.

“Watercourses run like ribbons through an area,” says Eline. “That means nature-friendly banks can also help to connect habitats with one another. For many species, these are valuable routes through the landscape.”

In addition, nature-friendly banks can contribute to water quality. Plants provide structure, shelter and nutrient uptake. This results in a healthier, more natural water system.

Combining opportunities with existing work

Many municipalities already have a dredging schedule, a maintenance task or a task involving bank protection. These are where the NVO tool fits in perfectly.

“If you are already working on the watercourse, it makes sense to look straight away at whether you can also do something for biodiversity and water quality,” she says. “With the tool, we can show where there may be room for a nature-friendly bank. That way, you can include sustainable opportunities in work that is already planned.”

That makes the tool interesting for clients who want to make their dredging plan or bank management more sustainable. Not as a separate project alongside existing work, but as a smart addition to the management task. In this way, a regular schedule suddenly also becomes an opportunity to realise more nature-friendly banks step by step.

Making smarter use of existing data

The NVO tool combines several data sources. Think of elevation data, topographic information, parcel ownership and obstacles such as roads, buildings and trees. It also looks at the space available along the water and, where possible, at the profile of the watercourse itself.

This produces an initial picture. It shows where there is little or no space, where an adapted layout may offer opportunities, and where there appears to be sufficient space for a nature-friendly bank. The tool therefore does not provide a final design, but it does offer a practical starting point for further assessment.

“It is above all a way of providing direction more quickly,” Eline explains. “You don’t have to assess every watercourse manually from scratch. The tool helps you see where to focus your attention.”

The tool provides direction, the adviser makes the judgement

Creating a nature-friendly bank remains tailor-made work. Sometimes a location appears less suitable based on data, while in practice there are still possibilities. For example, because a small interruption caused by trees can be resolved with a different layout. Conversely, a location may look promising on paper, but practice calls for additional consideration.

What about stability? Is there enough space? Does the measure fit within the management regime? Which design is both ecologically valuable and technically feasible? And how can maintenance be carried out in a nature-friendly way?

“The insight of an ecologist on site remains important,” says Eline. “The tool identifies promising locations. Afterwards, we assess what is really possible technically, ecologically and in terms of management.”

From opportunity map to implementation

For clients, promising locations can be shown on the map alongside existing management information and the dredging schedule. This makes it clear at a glance where planned work can potentially be combined with the creation of nature-friendly banks.

If a location is promising, our advisers will help with the next steps: from further inspection, ecological assessment and design to permits, specifications, tendering and supervision during implementation.

Looking ahead with data, ecology and management

With the tool, we work together to make sustainability ambitions concrete within existing activities. Not by treating nature-friendly banks as a stand-alone wish, but by including them at the moment when choices are already being made about dredging, maintenance or the replacement of piling.

The first responses are enthusiastic and give clients the opportunity to supplement their own dredging planning or maintenance programme with the tool. The possibilities for nature-friendly banks are recorded on a (digital) map that can easily be added to the systems currently in use.

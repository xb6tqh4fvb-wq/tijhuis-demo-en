Sloterstrand: hundreds of samples for better bathing water

01 Project › 02 Research › 03 Water bed › 04 Measures › 05 KWR › 06 Next steps ›

The project

Sloterstrand is a popular spot on the Sloterplas in Amsterdam. Yet water quality has been under pressure in recent years. Due to excessive E. coli levels, the beach lost its official status as a bathing location.

Since spring 2024, the City of Amsterdam and Tijhuis Ingenieurs have been investigating together what exactly is happening in and around the bathing area. Where do the bacteria come from? Why are the problems mainly occurring here? And which measures actually have an effect?

The strength of this project lies in combining fieldwork, rapid analyses and expert interpretation. By measuring at fixed times and at different locations, insight into the behaviour of E. coli at this specific location is built up step by step.

From source investigation to course of action

When E. coli levels are elevated, the first question is: does the contamination come from outside, or does the problem arise at the location itself? That is why measurements are taken not only in the swimming area, but also at locations around Sloterstrand and in other parts of the Sloterplas.

With mobile analysis equipment from the City of Amsterdam, samples can be analysed within approximately 14 hours. This makes far more intensive monitoring possible than is often the case with regular research. In 2024, around 400 water samples were taken, in 2025 around 300, and in 2026 the count already stands at more than 250 samples and still counting.

This series of measurements made patterns visible. The elevated E. coli levels turned out not to be carried in from the rest of the Sloterplas, but to be linked mainly to Sloterstrand itself. The question thus shifted from 'where does it come from?' to 'why does it remain present specifically here?'

The water bed as an important piece of the puzzle

An important part of the research consists of disturbance tests. In these, the water bed is deliberately stirred up, similar to what happens through swimmers, animals or waves.

These tests show that E. coli levels can increase markedly after the sediment has been disturbed. This indicates that bacteria can accumulate in the water bed and re-enter the bathing water when the bed is disturbed.

The situation is therefore more complex than just a source from outside. The layout and flow of the swimming area, use by birds and recreational visitors, and the properties of the water bed all play a role. It is precisely by looking at these factors together that a better picture of possible measures emerges.

Testing measures in practice

On the basis of the research results, the City of Amsterdam has taken measures. One of these was the removal of the mussel screen in front of the beach. This screen had previously been installed as a measure against blue-green algae, but it also reduced flow in the swimming area. With the screen removed, the water can be exchanged more effectively with the rest of the Sloterplas.

In addition, bird droppings along the waterline are cleared away daily. Birds, and geese in particular, can contribute to faecal contamination. By removing droppings before they enter the water, a possible source of E. coli is reduced.

The monitoring then shows what these measures achieve in practice. In 2026, water quality is checked three times a week during the swimming season. In this way, the City of Amsterdam keeps track of E. coli levels and can inform swimmers about current bathing water quality.

Collaboration with KWR

Not all questions can be answered directly through regular monitoring. That is why this project also involves collaboration with KWR Water Research Institute, a knowledge institute for applied water research.

Together, they are investigating how long E. coli can survive in the water bed. This includes examining whether the bacterium also remains present in winter and can flourish again in spring. This knowledge is important for determining longer-term measures.

If E. coli can persist in the sediment all year round, this calls for a different approach than if the bacterium accumulates in the water bed anew each time. The project is therefore not only about measuring what is happening today, but also about understanding what happens in the different seasons.

From measuring to better-informed choices

For Tijhuis Ingenieurs, this project is distinctive in terms of content. Many projects focus on the water bed, dredging and preparation for implementation. At Sloterstrand, the emphasis is instead on long-term monitoring of surface water quality.

This approach not only provides insight for this location, but also valuable experience for similar issues. Think of swimming locations where E. coli, blue-green algae or other water quality problems occur and where managers need fast, reliable information to make decisions.

Sloterstrand shows how important it is not to view measurements in isolation from the place where they are carried out. By looking at the layout, flow, water bed and use by birds and recreational visitors together, we gain a firmer grip, step by step, on a complex issue: how do you keep a popular recreation spot accessible as safely and responsibly as possible?
